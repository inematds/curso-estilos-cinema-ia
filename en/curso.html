Build a reference board with a purpose

You can organize up to nine references without mixing their roles.

Reading: about 15 minutes. Practice: 10 active minutes. Queues, reference production, and corrections take extra time.

A beautiful collage can misguide a scene. Each reference should show what it provides: object, setting, light, or visual language.

In 1 minute

  1. Case: fictional Vagalume lantern
  2. Roles: object, setting, light, and style
  3. Selection: a few files with clear purposes

1A reference is a visible decision

Reference is material that guides a choice.

In the Vagalume case, a yellow lantern appears in a forest at dusk.

Separate the object, setting, lighting, and framing images.

If every image shows something different without a purpose, the generator receives an ambiguous set.

Lara wants to compare two visual languages for the same short ad.

Roberto needs viewers to recognize the lantern and the setting of his inn.

They build a board of up to nine images, but send only those needed for the test.

The number is an organization exercise, not a universal requirement.

Lara, a 36-year-old art director, explains each image’s purpose.

2A tool limit is not a creative goal

The original Seedance 2.0 documentation describes text, image, video, and audio as inputs.

The 2.5 line expands the limits and controls.

The platform where you use the model may offer different combinations.

Check the version, format, cost, and accepted quantity in your account.

Nine images on the board do not mean you must send nine files.

One clear image can do more than several repeated ones.

Keep the organized version to compare decisions, and record which references were actually attached during generation.

Roberto, a 58-year-old innkeeper, chooses his own photo when he needs to show his actual equipment.

3Check the decision against its use

Allowed quantity does not prove quality; each file must resolve a project decision.

Roberto uses the checklist to confirm: selection: a few files with clear purposes.

Describe two styles through observable traits

You can write two style notes and prepare starting images.

Reading: about 15 minutes. Practice: 10 active minutes. Queues, reference production, and corrections take extra time.

A style name alone can hide important choices. Define shape, texture, color, and light so you can review the result.

In 1 minute

  1. Style A: 2D, outlines, flat colors
  2. Style B: layered paper, tactile edges
  3. Keep fixed: object, story, and main palette

1Five visual languages, different choices

2D animation can use outlines and flat colors.

Pixel art builds shapes from visible blocks.

A 3D scene can explore volume and materials.

Low-poly style recalls older games, such as those from the PS1 era.

Voxel art uses cubic volumes, familiar from games such as Minecraft.

In a paper model, cutouts form layers, and small shadows show the space between them.

These names do not require copying game characters or settings.

Describe the visual language you want using original elements.

For Vagalume, we will compare flat-color 2D animation with a layered paper model.

The lantern and story stay the same.

Lara describes the texture instead of relying only on a style name.

2The starting image must match the style note

Prepare one version of the object for each style.

You can draw it or use an instruction-based image editor, such as a compatible Nano Banana mode.

Provide your reference and ask to change the visual language while preserving the shape and key parts.

Review it before animating.

A style change can alter the product.

Compare the lantern’s handle, body, and base.

If a feature disappears, correct the starting image.

A style note does not guarantee that a missing detail will return.

Keep the original reference with both versions.

Roberto checks whether the lantern still has the handle shown in the reference.

3Check the decision against its use

Style review includes checking that the object is still recognizable, not just that the texture looks good.

Roberto uses the checklist to confirm: keep fixed: object, story, and main palette.

Write three shots that tell the same story

You can build a short storyboard with a beginning, action, and outcome.

Reading: about 15 minutes. Practice: 10 active minutes. Queues, reference production, and corrections take extra time.

Two styles are easy to compare only when they tell the same story. Set the sequence before choosing effects.

In 1 minute

  1. Shot 1: introduce the object and setting
  2. Shot 2: show the change in light
  3. Shot 3: reveal the result

1Each shot should add information

A storyboard is a visual script made of frames.

One shot shows the lantern in its setting; another shows the action; the third reveals its light.

Note the action, camera, and ending point in each frame.

Add up the durations to fit the total allowed by the mode.

To make it easier, use one simple action and a subtle camera move.

A hand pressing a button may need more corrections than a planned change in light.

Choose what needs to appear, and check that your reference shows that part of the object.

Lara keeps the prompt focused to spot conflicts before spending.

2A short text helps reveal conflicts

Organize the context, references, shots, style, and audio.

Use up to one thousand five hundred characters as the editorial limit for this exercise.

That number is not a universal Seedance restriction.

If you need to shorten it, remove repeated adjectives before essential actions.

Write the transitions: simple cut, continuous push-in, or a match cut between similar shapes.

Do not ask for a locked camera and a large camera move at the same time.

Versions A and B use the same sequence; only the style note and matching image change.

Roberto keeps the same story in both versions to compare the message.

3Check the decision against its use

A and B should tell the same story so the comparison highlights the style change.

Roberto uses the checklist to confirm: shot 3: reveal the result.

Plan the camera, lens, and story moment

You can write a camera sheet with an intent and review criterion.

Reading: about 15 minutes. Practice: 10 active minutes. Queues, reference production, and corrections take extra time.

Camera and lens names do not replace an intent. Decide what the audience needs to notice, then choose the framing.

In 1 minute

  1. Purpose: reveal the light turning on
  2. Camera: still or a short push-in
  3. Criterion: readable object and clear action

1Framing guides attention

A wide shot shows how the object relates to its surroundings.

A close-up highlights a detail.

Focal length describes a lens characteristic that affects the viewing angle.

With the same sensor format, a shorter focal length usually shows a wider field of view.

Camera distance also changes perspective.

Twenty-four millimeters may suggest a wide field of view in some setups, but it is not a universal filmmaking formula.

In a generator, this control is a visual guide; assess the image it produces.

Do not treat the selected name as a physical measurement from a real shoot.

Lara chooses camera movement based on the story moment, not a list of effects.

2Movement should serve the event

To show the flashlight turning on, a still camera can make the change clear.

A slow push-in can highlight the lit window.

If the goal is to show the place, a wider view can reveal the forest.

Choose one movement and one end point.

In Cinema Studio, camera, lens, and light controls depend on the version and mode.

Record the options you actually used.

Start with a simple setup to learn how it affects the result.

Add complexity only when it solves a story need.

Roberto wants enough space to recognize the inn’s surroundings.

3Check the decision against its use

The lens name does not prove the result; check how the object and setting appear in the video.

Roberto uses the worksheet to check: criterion: readable object and clear action.

  3. Review: watch the whole transition
